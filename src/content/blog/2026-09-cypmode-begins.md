In September 2026 I started CYPMode. It began as a narrow question about CYP inhibition labels and grew into my entry for the OpenADMET CYP Inhibition Blind Challenge, which I describe in later posts.

Cytochrome P450 enzymes metabolise most drugs, and inhibiting one of them is a common cause of drug–drug interactions. A binary inhibition label, as found in public benchmarks such as the Therapeutics Data Commons sets, records that a compound inhibits an isoform in an assay. It does not say how. The how matters, because there are different mechanisms. A compound can occupy the substrate pocket and compete with other drugs, or it can bind the heme iron at the centre of the enzyme through a nitrogen atom, a so-called Type II interaction, which tends to be strong and persistent. Many well-known inhibitors, such as the azole antifungals, work in the second way.

My starting question was simple. How many of the compounds labelled as inhibitors have any structural motif that could coordinate the heme iron? If a large share of positives did, then a model could learn the motif and the label would be largely explained by it. If a small share did, then the labels must come from other chemistry and a heme-based explanation would cover only part of the picture. I screened the TDC inhibition labels for heme-coordinating motifs and found that only 26 to 31% of the labelled inhibitors, among 2,514 to 5,829 compounds per isoform, carry any such motif. That result set the tone of the project: heme coordination is real and important, but it explains a minority of labels, so a model needs more than a heme rule.

The result also shaped how I thought about the challenge. A heme-coordinating nitrogen is a physical feature that can be written down as a geometry: an iron-to-nitrogen distance around 2.1 to 2.2 Å in the crystal structures that I use on the Visualisation page. A statistical model sees only the label, but a structure model can in principle see the contact. That is the reason the project has two halves, a predictive stack for time-dependent inhibition and a structure recipe for the CYP3A4 track, with a restraint that pulls a ligand nitrogen toward the iron.

I also set the working rules at the start. Every idea would have to pass a gate written before its result, so that I could not choose a threshold after seeing which one looked best. Every reported number would have to be regenerated by an audit script. Every negative result would also be recorded in the repository. Those rules are why the project has 187 pre-registered verdicts, 9 of them met and 178 not, and a public ledger of the rest.

I released the code under the AGPL-3.0-or-later licence and the documents under CC BY-NC-SA, with the generated data under CC BY. The repository is at github.com/mohamedtalaat-gif/CYPMode, and a later post describes the archived release that carries a DOI.

A model trained on a biased sample inherits the bias, so the way compounds were selected for testing matters as much as the labels. That concern led to the selection-aware CYP2D6 model that I report on the Model validation page.
