In 2017 I completed the Medicinal Chemistry Research Internship Program (MCRI), provided by the Department of Pharmaceutical Chemistry at the Faculty of Pharmacy, Ain Shams University. It ran during two periods of 2017, from 26 January to 5 February, in the mid-semester break, and from 1 July to 16 September, in the summer vacation. The certificate describes it as hands-on training in synthetic organic chemistry and medicinal chemistry, through taking an effective part in an ongoing research project on the development of antimicrobial agents.

A pharmacy curriculum teaches the drug as a finished object: a structure, a mechanism, a dose. A medicinal chemistry laboratory teaches how that object comes to exist. A lead compound is rarely the final answer. It is one point in a series of analogues, and each analogue answers a narrow question about structure and activity. Which substituent can leave the ring without losing potency? Which group raises solubility? Which change makes the molecule easier to make in the quantity a biological assay needs? The chemistry is in the answers, and the answers come slowly, one flask at a time.

An antimicrobial project makes these questions concrete. Activity is measured against an organism, so potency has a direct, readable meaning, and the compounds have to reach their target through a cell wall or membrane. That ties the structure to physical properties such as lipophilicity, charge and size, which are the same properties that decide absorption and distribution later in a drug's life. I did not know then how much of my later work would revolve around exactly this link between structure and fate in the body, but the internship was the first place where I handled it as a laboratory problem and not as a lecture.

The program also taught the habits of synthetic work. A reaction is planned on paper, run, followed, worked up and purified, and then the product has to be shown to be what the plan said it was. Nothing is accepted on trust, because an impure or mis-assigned compound makes every downstream measurement meaningless. I now apply the same rule to code and models: a number is not a result until it has been regenerated from the data and checked against something independent. The audit scripts in my repositories are the computational form of that bench discipline.

A third lesson was about how projects are organised. A research group's chemistry is split across people: someone designs the series, someone makes the compounds, someone tests them, and the results feed the next design. A student on such a project is one link in that loop, and the loop only works if records are exact enough for another person to repeat a step. That expectation of traceable records carried over directly into formulation work and then into the way I document computational experiments.

The program is listed in the education section of my CV, and the certificate is in my records. It is a short entry for a part of my training that I use more than its length suggests. When I read a structure–activity table in a paper now, or look at where a ligand sits against a heme iron in a crystal structure, I am reading it with some memory of how hard each row of that table was to produce. That memory makes me more careful about what a prediction can claim, and more respectful of the experiments that give models anything to learn from.

I came away from the internship intending to keep one foot in the laboratory whatever else I learned, and that is how the years since have gone: formulation at the bench, computation beside it, and the same insistence that both are checked against measurements.
