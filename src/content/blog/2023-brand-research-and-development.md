In July 2023 I started as an R&D researcher at BRAND for Pharmaceutical Industries in Giza, and I stayed until July 2024. It was my first full-time position in industrial formulation, and it taught me how a formulation behaves when it leaves the bench.

My work covered four kinds of task. I ran formulation trials under good laboratory practice, carried out stability studies, supported scale-up transfers from the laboratory to production, and prepared registration files and product specification sheets for the Ministry of Health. Each of them looks at the same product from a different side, and together they show why industrial development is more than recipe writing.

A formulation trial asks whether a combination of active ingredient and excipients gives a product with the intended properties. In a tablet that may mean hardness, disintegration and dissolution. In a liquid it may mean clarity, viscosity and pH. A trial is only meaningful if it is documented and repeatable, which is why it runs under good laboratory practice: materials, quantities, equipment and results all have to be recorded so that another person could repeat the batch.

Stability studies ask how long the product keeps those properties. A formulation that is right on the day it is made may change over months: the active can degrade, the physical form can shift, the packaging can interact with the contents. Studies under controlled temperature and humidity conditions show how fast that happens and set the shelf life, and they have to be designed before the batches are made, with the tests and acceptance limits fixed in advance. I recognise the same logic in the pre-registered gates I use for my models today.

Scale-up transfers move a process from small laboratory batches to production equipment. The difficulty is that the physics changes with size. Mixing times, heat transfer, shear and drying behave differently in a large vessel, so a formula that works in a beaker can fail in a plant. A researcher on a transfer works with production staff, adjusts parameters, and compares the first production batches to the laboratory reference. I took part in a small number of these transfers, and watching a formula meet real equipment was the most instructive part of the year.

The registration files bring everything together in writing. A file for the Ministry of Health sets out the composition, the manufacturing process, the controls, the stability data and the specification of the finished product. Writing it forces a researcher to check that every claim is supported by a record, which is a good habit for any scientific author.

The year at BRAND also shaped my interest in prediction. Many trials in a formulation department are run because nobody can say beforehand which excipient, which ratio or which process setting will work. If even part of that could be predicted from molecular and colloidal properties, fewer batches would be wasted. That observation is the practical origin of the delivery-system and surfactant work that I released in 2026, and it is why I describe my research as computational pharmaceutics grounded in industrial experience.

I moved on to a project leadership role at Label Rock in December 2024, which I describe in a separate post.
