During 2020 I completed six online courses alongside my degree. Five were on Coursera and one, on pharmacovigilance, came from the Uppsala Monitoring Centre. The Coursera certificates can be verified online, and the links are on the Activities page.

The first was "How to Write and Publish a Scientific Paper" from École Polytechnique. A pharmacy degree trains you to read papers, and almost never to write one. The course treats a manuscript as an argument with a fixed shape: a question that matters, a method that could answer it, results that stand apart from interpretation, and a discussion that says what follows and what does not. That is the shape I have followed in my sole-author paper in 2024 and in the scoping review I led in 2025.

The second, "Programming for Everybody (Getting Started with Python)" from the University of Michigan, is the one with the longest reach. It taught the basics: variables, loops, functions, files, and data structures. Everything I have released since is written in Python, from the cheminformatics pipelines to the validation audits. The course did not make me a programmer, but it removed the first barrier and gave me a language in which to keep learning.

Two courses from Johns Hopkins University, "Introduction to Genomic Technologies" and "Genomic Data Science with Galaxy", moved in a different direction. They covered how sequencing data are produced and how they are analysed in a workflow platform without writing code. I do not work on genomic data now, but the courses were my first view of an analysis pipeline as a series of explicit, repeatable steps whose parameters are recorded. That idea of a recorded, repeatable workflow is the same one my repositories rely on.

"Mindshift: Break Through Obstacles to Learning" from McMaster University, completed with honors, is about learning itself: how to approach unfamiliar subjects, how memory and practice interact, and how to overcome the sense that a subject is not for you. It was useful to me because the work I wanted to do spans several fields, and learning across fields is a skill in its own right.

The pharmacovigilance course from the Uppsala Monitoring Centre sits closer to pharmacy. Pharmacovigilance is the monitoring of the safety of medicines after they reach patients: collecting reports of adverse reactions, judging whether a drug caused them, and acting on the signals. It fits alongside the preclinical side of my interests, because it shows what happens to a compound after the models and the trials are finished, and why the earlier stages try to predict problems such as drug–drug interactions before they occur.

I list these courses on the site for completeness and not for weight. They are not research, and the CV leaves them out. They are, however, the way I acquired tools that later work depended on: a language to write code in, a structure to write papers in, a way to think about pipelines, and a sense of where safety monitoring takes over. Taken together they shaped the shift from a purely pharmacy-based education toward the computational work in this section of the site.
