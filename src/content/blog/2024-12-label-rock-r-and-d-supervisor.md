In December 2024 I became R&D Supervisor and Project Lead at Label Rock, a private-label pharmaceutical and cosmetic manufacturer in Cairo. It is a full-time position, and I hold it today. It is the role in which my formulation work is the most varied.

A private-label manufacturer develops and produces products that are sold under a client's brand. Each client brings a request: a dosage form, a market, a price point, sometimes a reference product to match. The R&D department turns the request into a formula, a process, and a dossier, and then makes the product at pilot and commercial scale. That structure means I work on many projects at once, across solid, liquid, and parenteral dosage forms, and each one runs from pre-formulation and compatibility studies through to scale-up.

The first stage of a project is knowing the active ingredient and its limits. Pre-formulation looks at solubility, stability, particle properties, and compatibility with candidate excipients, because the choices made here constrain everything afterwards. Compatibility testing is a good example: two ingredients that look harmless on paper can react during storage, and it is cheaper to find that out on a bench than in a stability chamber after the batches are made. From there the work moves to trial batches, adjustment of the formula, stability studies and, finally, scale-up to production equipment.

My own role is a mixture of technical ownership and leadership. I own the projects, which means I am responsible for the formula, the experimental plan, and the decision to proceed. I lead a team of senior researchers, which means distributing the work, reviewing the results, and making sure the records are good enough for another person to repeat a batch. I author the master batch records, which set out exactly how a product is made, and the CTD sections and regulatory text that go into submissions. I also resolve deviations on the production floor, such as a batch that behaves differently from the pilot, under good manufacturing practice and standard operating procedures.

The role has made me value documentation more than I did as a student. A master batch record is a form of specification: it says what will be done and what will be accepted, before anything is made. A CTD section is a form of argument: it says what was done and presents the evidence. Both are written for readers who will check them. I now use the same pattern for computational work, with pre-registered gates in place of acceptance criteria and an audit in place of a reviewer's check.

It has also sharpened my view of where prediction would help. A project manager in a private-label company wants to know early whether a formula will be stable, whether a surfactant system will hold an emulsion, and how many trial batches to plan. Those answers currently come from experience and from repeating experiments. My research asks whether some of them can come from models of the molecules and colloids involved, so that fewer batches are needed.
