On 19 January 2024 I attended the 16th edition of the Breast Gynecological International Cancer Conference (BGICC) in Cairo. I followed the Pfizer "Meet the Experts" breast workshop and the "Creation and Innovation" session. I went as a participant, and the conference is listed under conferences and seminars on my Activities page.

Breast cancer is one of the diseases in which treatment has moved furthest from a single standard. The tumour is classified by its biology, such as hormone receptor status and HER2 expression, and the classification decides which medicines are used and in what order. Surgery, radiotherapy, hormonal agents, targeted drugs, and chemotherapy are combined in sequences that depend on the stage and subtype. A conference that brings clinicians, pharmacists, and industry together on this subject is a place to see how those sequences are being revised, and to hear where the open questions are.

The workshop format, "Meet the Experts", is built around discussion. A small group puts questions to specialists on practical problems, such as how to manage a combination, what to do when a patient cannot tolerate a dose, or how new evidence changes a protocol. For a pharmacist this is directly useful, because the pharmacist is the person who checks that the protocol on the order is the one the evidence supports, prepares the doses, and watches for interactions. It is the same attention to the details of an order that I apply to chemotherapy preparation, and I started that work at El Demerdash Hospital in the same year.

The title of the "Creation and Innovation" session is a good starting point for a thought, because the word innovation is used loosely in oncology. A new drug is the visible form of it, but much of the progress that reaches patients is about how existing drugs are delivered and combined: a different schedule, a new formulation, a way of reaching the tumour with a smaller dose. That is the part of innovation that connects to my own interests. Delivery systems for anticancer drugs, from liposomes to nanoparticles, are among the most studied examples of formulation changing what a drug can do, and my scoping review of nano-antioxidants in 2025 comes from the same concern with how a drug reaches its target.

A conference also shows the distance between a molecule and a patient. A clinical conference is about people and decisions, with a protocol and a dose at the end, and that is a useful corrective for someone who spends part of the week with models and structures. When I think about what a prediction would have to do to matter, the answer lies in rooms like that one: it would have to fit into a decision that a clinician or a pharmacist actually makes, with the evidence and the constraints at hand.

I list the conference with the other events I attended. Attendance is a modest entry, and I describe it as what it was: a day of listening to specialists in a field where my hospital work and my research interests meet.
