From 2021 to 2023 I served as an emergency and clinical pharmacist at the Egyptian Military Medical Unit, 3rd Field Army Headquarters, in Suez. The post was my mandatory national service after graduation. When it ended I worked in a community pharmacy in Cairo as a pharmacist and shift manager.

Emergency pharmacy is pharmacy under time pressure. A patient arrives, a prescriber orders a medicine, and the pharmacist has to supply the right drug in the right form and dose, often with little warning. The work rests on a few disciplines that do not change with the setting: check the order against the patient, check the dose against the indication, check the product against the order, and record what was dispensed. A mistake in any of those steps can reach a patient quickly, so the checks are made routine and habitual.

Across the two positions my duties included dispensing, controlled-drug registers, and vaccination and screening clinics. A clinic serves many people in a short time with a limited range of procedures, so the pharmacist's role is to keep supplies correct, store vaccines under the right conditions, and make sure that each step is documented. Cold-chain discipline is a good example. A vaccine is a formulation that loses activity if it is warmed or frozen outside its range, and the person handling it is the last control in the chain. The ideas of stability and storage conditions that I studied in pharmaceutics became a daily practical matter.

Controlled drugs added a further layer of record keeping. Registers have to balance exactly, every transaction has to be signed, and discrepancies have to be explained. The principle is the same one I value in research: records exist so that someone else can check what was done. I also learned how much of clinical pharmacy is communication, with physicians about orders, with nurses about administration, and with patients about use.

I took the period seriously as training even though it was outside research. A hospital or military unit pharmacy shows how medicines are used in practice, with the awkward constraints that real use brings: patients who cannot swallow, products that are out of stock, doses that need adjusting. A formulation scientist who has seen that side designs differently. When I now think about delivery systems for patients with particular needs, the questions come from that experience: whether the dose can be given reliably, whether the product survives storage in a hot climate, and whether a pharmacist can prepare and check it quickly.

The community pharmacy role that followed in 2023 added the responsibility of managing a shift. I list both positions in the experience section of my CV with brief descriptions. They fill the years between graduation and my move into formulation research and development, and they are part of why my work stays anchored in how medicines are actually supplied.
