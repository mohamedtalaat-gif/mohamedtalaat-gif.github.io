In 2024 I completed the organic skincare and organic haircare formulation diplomas at Formula Botanica. They are online programmes for cosmetic formulators, and I took them to add cosmetic science to a pharmaceutical background.

Pharmaceutical and cosmetic formulation share a great deal and differ in the details that matter. Both turn active and functional ingredients into a stable, pleasant, safe product. In pharmaceutics the active is a drug with a defined therapeutic effect, and the regulatory standard is set by that effect. In cosmetics the claims are about appearance, feel and care, the ingredient lists are very different, and consumers judge the product directly through texture, scent and behaviour on the skin or hair. A formulator who moves between the two has to hold both sets of expectations.

Courses of this kind cover the structure of cosmetic products from the ground up. Emulsions are the central example. A lotion or cream is a dispersion of oil and water stabilised by emulsifiers, and its stability depends on the type and amount of emulsifier, the order of mixing, the temperature, and the other ingredients present. Thickeners and polymers set the rheology, which the user experiences as how a cream spreads and how it feels. Surfactant systems in shampoos and cleansers have their own logic, because cleaning power, foam, mildness and viscosity pull against each other and have to be balanced.

Preservation and safety are part of the subject. Any water-containing product supports microbial growth, so a preservative system has to be chosen, tested and kept within limits that are effective and tolerated. Ingredient compatibility, pH and the effect of the packaging all enter the design. These are the same questions as in a pharmaceutical topical product, handled with a different set of permitted ingredients.

Organic and natural formulation adds constraints. Natural materials vary from batch to batch, their performance can be harder to standardise, and some common synthetic ingredients are excluded by certification rules. A formulator working in this space learns to compensate with processing, with ingredient selection and with testing. I found this a useful complement to my academic work on natural-ingredient formulations.

The reason I include the diplomas on this site is that they connect directly to my wet-lab work in private-label manufacturing and to the computational work on surfactants. Cosmetic formulations are rich in surfactants and polymers, and their behaviour is where coarse-grained simulation can help: micelle size, packing and assembly all influence how a cleanser performs. The surfactant notebook that I released in 2026 started as a way to understand that behaviour in a personal-care setting. Knowing how a formulator uses these materials makes the simulations more useful to me, because I know which properties a formulator would want them to predict.

The diplomas are listed in the education section of my CV. They are short courses by comparison with a degree, and I treat them as what they are: practical training in a part of formulation that my pharmacy degree did not cover.
