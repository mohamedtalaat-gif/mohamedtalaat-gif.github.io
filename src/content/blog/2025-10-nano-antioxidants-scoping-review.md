title: A scoping review of nano-antioxidants for neurodegenerative disorders
date: "2025-10-29"
summary: First-author scoping review in Discover Nano of 171 preclinical studies on nanocarriers for antioxidants in neurodegenerative disease, with gaps in nanoplatforms and comparative designs.
topic: paper
---

On 29 October 2025 Discover Nano published "Nano-antioxidants for neurodegenerative disorders: a scoping review" (volume 20, article 194). I am the first author, and the paper was written with ten co-authors. Its DOI is 10.1186/s11671-025-04320-9.

The review begins with a gap between promise and practice. Antioxidants help treat or prevent neurodegenerative diseases in many experimental settings, since oxidative stress is part of how neurons are damaged. The difficulty is delivery. The blood–brain barrier limits what gets into the brain, so an antioxidant with good activity in a dish can have poor bioavailability in the central nervous system. Nano drug delivery systems offer a way across the barrier and a way to raise antioxidant activity at the target. A large number of preclinical studies exist, yet none of them has reached clinical practice.

A scoping review is the right tool for such a field. It does not pool results into a single effect, as a meta-analysis does. It maps the literature: how many studies there are, what they investigated, how they were designed, and where the gaps lie. The review followed the PRISMA-ScR guidelines and searched PubMed for primary studies on nano-antioxidants in neurodegenerative disorders over the last five years. For each study the year, the contributing affiliations, the type of research, the antioxidant agent, and the delivery matrix were extracted, and the choice of delivery system was related to year and country of publication.

The analysis included 171 studies, and every one of them was preclinical. Organic and lipid-based nanocarriers dominated, at 31.95% and 26.04% of the studies. Nanoplatforms, which are more complex engineered structures, appeared in only one study. Only 24 studies, 14.04% of the total, used a comparative design, meaning that they tested the nanoformulation against a conventional form or against another carrier. Without that comparison it is hard to say whether a nanocarrier adds anything, so the lack is a real weakness of the field.

The conclusion was that nanoplatforms and comparative designs are underrepresented, and that the field needs rigorous validation systems and methodological approaches to predict what happens to these carriers *in vivo*. That sentence is a fair summary of my research direction. The questions that remain open are questions of prediction and validation: which carrier properties determine its fate, how to test a claim against an appropriate comparator, and how to report the results so that others can build on them.

Leading the review taught me a great deal about process. A scoping review is a project in its own right, with a protocol, a search, screening decisions, extraction forms, and a way to resolve disagreement among reviewers. A review with eleven authors needs agreed definitions at every step. The result also made me careful about what a count of studies can say. A figure such as 31.95% describes the literature, not the carriers' merit, and the paper is explicit about that.

The review is listed on my publications page, on Google Scholar, and in my ORCID record. It is the nearest thing in my publications to the questions in my current work, and it explains why the validation reports on this site stress comparators, held-out data, and intervals.
