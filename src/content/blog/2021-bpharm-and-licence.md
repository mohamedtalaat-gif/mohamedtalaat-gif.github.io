In 2021 I finished my Bachelor of Pharmacy in Pharmaceutical Sciences at the Faculty of Pharmacy, Ain Shams University in Cairo, and registered as a certified pharmacist with the Egyptian Pharmacists Syndicate. The degree is the foundation for everything else on this site, and the licence is what lets me practise.

The degree covers the sciences around a medicine: chemistry, pharmacology, pharmaceutics, pharmacognosy, analysis, and clinical practice. For a student interested in how drugs behave, its value lies in how these subjects interlock. A drug's structure decides its properties. Its properties decide how it can be formulated. The formulation decides how much reaches the site of action, and pharmacology and clinical practice decide what that amount does. A pharmacist is trained to hold all of those links in mind at once, and that habit is what I later wanted to express quantitatively.

During the degree I took the opportunities that fitted my interests. The Medicinal Chemistry Research Internship in 2017 gave me bench experience in synthesis, the Volatile Oil Gallery award in 2019 confirmed that formulation was a field I wanted to work in, and the lung-targeted rifampicin microparticle project in 2020 gave me particle engineering and regulatory documentation. I also completed online courses in scientific writing, Python, and genomic data analysis. Each of these is described in its own post.

Registration with the Syndicate is the formal step that follows graduation. In Egypt a pharmacist must be registered with the Syndicate to practise, whether in a community pharmacy, a hospital, a manufacturer, or a regulatory role. The licence is the basis for the positions I have held since: emergency and clinical pharmacy during my national service, formulation research and development in industry, and chemotherapy preparation in a hospital. It also carries responsibilities that a degree alone does not, such as the duty to dispense and counsel correctly and to keep controlled-drug records accurately.

Looking back, the main thing the degree gave me is breadth, and the main thing it left to me is depth. A graduate who has seen all the parts of the field is well placed to decide which part to go deeper into. My choice was the point where formulation and molecular properties meet, because that is where a prediction would be most useful to someone making a product. I chose to build the computational skills for it in addition to the bench skills, and the work released in 2026 is the result.

I list the degree and the licence first in my education section for a practical reason. Graduate programmes in computational pharmaceutics and cheminformatics read a pharmacy degree differently from a computer science or chemistry degree, and the difference is worth stating plainly. A pharmacist comes to modelling with knowledge of how the data are generated, what a formulation is, and where a prediction would be used. That context shapes which problems I pick and how I judge results, and it is the reason this site keeps its bench work next to its code.
