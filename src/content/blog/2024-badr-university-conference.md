In 2024 I attended the Badr University Conference of Pharmaceutical Sciences (BUCPS) in Badr, Egypt, and followed the session on machine learning methods in drug delivery: a novel pharmaceutics informatics approach. It is listed under conferences and seminars on my Activities page, and I describe here what a conference like that offers someone working between formulation and computation.

A pharmaceutical sciences conference in Egypt brings together academics, industry scientists, and students across the field: pharmaceutics, pharmaceutical chemistry, pharmacology, clinical pharmacy, and analysis. For a researcher whose work crosses those areas, the value lies in the breadth. In a single programme one can hear about a new excipient, a mechanism of drug resistance, and an analytical method, and see how people from different specialities frame the same problem. I find that useful because computational work tends to narrow one's view to datasets and metrics, and a conference restores the question of what a model is for.

The programme included machine learning in drug delivery, which is the area I was already moving toward. The topic shows how far the field has to go, and how much depends on the data. Delivery systems have many interacting variables, such as the composition of the carrier, the size of the particles, the surface charge, the drug loading, and the route, and published results are spread across papers that report them differently. A model that learns from such data is only as good as the consistency of the underlying measurements. That observation is at the heart of why my own releases insist on stated splits, intervals, and public failure records. A model of formulation outcomes is credible only when its evaluation is.

Conferences are also where I check my assumptions about what practitioners need. A formulator at a manufacturer does not want an abstract score. They want to know whether a surfactant mixture will be stable, whether a particle size will reach the target, and how many batches a prediction will save. Hearing the questions that industry and clinical colleagues ask keeps my projects pointed at problems that someone has.

Another benefit is meeting people. Research in Egypt often depends on informal networks, and a conference is a chance to meet researchers whose work overlaps with yours, whether in nanomedicine, formulation, or drug design. A conference gives a clearer map of who works on what in one's own country, which matters for the kind of collaboration I hope to build.

I attended as a participant and did not present, and this site describes it that way. A listing of attendance carries little weight on its own, and I include it for completeness and because the experience reinforced a direction. The conference confirmed that a gap exists between the speed at which computational methods advance and the pace at which pharmaceutics takes them up, and that the gap is best closed by people who understand both. That is the position I am working toward, and the papers, software, and validation reports on this site are my attempt to do it in the open.
