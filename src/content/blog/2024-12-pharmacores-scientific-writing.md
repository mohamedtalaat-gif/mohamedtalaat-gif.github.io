In December 2024 I started writing part time as a freelance scientific writer for PharmaCores, a knowledge platform. The assignment is scientific and pharmaceutical content, and the aim is to explain technical work to readers who are not specialists in it.

Writing for a general audience is a good test of understanding. A researcher who can state a result in the terms of the field can often do so without checking whether the underlying idea is clear. A reader outside the field gives no such allowance. The words have to carry the meaning on their own, which forces the writer to find out what the central point of a topic is and what can be left out without distortion. I have found that I understand a subject better after explaining it plainly than before.

The pharmaceutical setting makes accuracy matter more. A statement about a medicine can affect how people think about their treatment, so every claim has to rest on a source that I can name. The practice is to use primary literature and regulatory documents where possible, to state the limits of the evidence in the same sentence as the finding, and to avoid promising more than the data show. That is the same standard I apply to my own results, where a number is reported with its interval and its conditions.

The work also trains economy. A page for a platform has a length, an audience, and a purpose, and a good text selects. Choosing which mechanism to explain, which figure to quote, and which caveat to keep is an editorial skill that transfers directly to papers and to documentation. The READMEs in my repositories are written the same way: they say what the software does, what it does not do, and how to check it, in the order a newcomer needs.

Another benefit is range. A platform covers many topics, and writing on them keeps me in contact with fields I do not work in every day, such as clinical pharmacology, pharmacy practice, and new technologies in drug delivery. That breadth is useful in research, because ideas often come from a neighbouring field, and because collaborators come from them.

I keep this work separate from my research output. It is freelance and part time, it carries no publications, and my CV lists it as a short entry. I include it here because the skill is real and relevant. A computational pharmaceutics researcher spends as much time communicating results as producing them, and communication is something to practise deliberately. Each piece I write for PharmaCores is a chance to practise explaining a technical idea to someone who has not seen it before, which is the situation of every reader of a paper, a grant application, or a validation report.

The discipline has shaped this site as well. I tried to write each page so that a reader from pharmacy, chemistry, or computer science could follow it, with the technical detail available when it is wanted.
