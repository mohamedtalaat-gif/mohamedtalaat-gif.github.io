In 2024 I began working part time as an oncology and chemotherapy preparation pharmacist at El Demerdash Hospital, part of the Faculty of Medicine at Ain Shams University. I continue in the role alongside my formulation work.

The work centres on preparing cytotoxic medicines. A chemotherapy dose is calculated for each patient, usually from body surface area and the protocol, and the drug is diluted or reconstituted in a form that can be given. That is done under aseptic conditions, because the preparations go into the bloodstream of patients who are often immunocompromised. The environment, the technique, and the order of steps are all controlled to prevent microbial contamination. A second control protects the staff, since cytotoxic drugs are hazardous to handle, so preparation takes place in containment equipment with protective clothing and strict procedures for spills and waste.

Two disciplines run through the role. The first is exactness. A calculation error or a mislabelled syringe can harm a patient, so every dose is independently checked and every step is recorded. The second is sterility compliance, which means monitoring the preparation area, following cleaning schedules, and making sure that materials entering the clean area are handled correctly. I also manage the inventory of cytotoxic drugs: tracking stock, expiry dates, and storage conditions, because these medicines are costly and some have narrow stability windows after preparation.

The role includes counselling. Patients receiving chemotherapy face side effects, interactions with other medicines, and a complicated schedule, and a pharmacist working with the oncology and nursing teams can help by explaining the plan, checking for interactions, and flagging problems early. It is a reminder that a drug is not a molecule on a slide, but a treatment that a person has to take and live with.

Working in oncology pharmacy has influenced my research interests in a direct way. Chemotherapy is where the limits of conventional delivery are most visible. Many cytotoxic drugs distribute widely and harm healthy tissue, so a lot of effort goes into delivery systems that carry the drug more selectively, from liposomes to nanoparticles. My review of nano-antioxidants for neurodegenerative disorders and my work on delivery systems for the central nervous system come from the same concern, that getting the drug to the right place matters as much as the drug itself.

It also grounds my view of what a formulation must achieve in practice. A product that is stable for a day after preparation, that can be drawn up reliably, and that is compatible with the diluents and lines in use is easier to deliver safely than one that is not, regardless of how elegant its design is. Those practical requirements are easy to miss from a laboratory bench, and I consider the hospital work a useful check on my formulation thinking.
