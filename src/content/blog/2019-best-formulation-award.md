In 2019 a formulation I developed as an undergraduate won the Best Formulation Award at the 6th Volatile Oil Gallery, held at the Faculty of Pharmacy, Ain Shams University. The formula was a topical product for eczema and burns, based on a natural active ingredient.

A topical product for skin conditions raises a set of questions that a tablet never does. The skin is a barrier, and what reaches it must be delivered in a vehicle that spreads, stays where it is placed, tolerates the skin's surface and keeps its active ingredient stable. A natural active ingredient adds a further layer. Plant-derived materials are mixtures, their composition varies with source and processing, and many of their components are volatile or sensitive to light, heat and oxygen. A formulator has to decide what is actually the active part, how to keep it in the product for months, and how to show that the finished product behaves the same way from one batch to the next.

Those questions shaped the work. A vehicle for eczema and burns has to be gentle on damaged skin, so the choice of base and excipients matters as much as the active. Emulsion-type bases, gels and ointments each trade occlusion, spreadability and water content against each other. Preservation is a real constraint, because a water-containing product on broken skin needs protection from microbial growth that does not itself irritate. Stability has to be checked against the conditions the product will meet, including temperature changes during storage and use.

What I took from the award was less the prize than the structure of the problem. A formulation is a set of decisions that interact, and no single decision can be judged alone. Changing the emulsifier changes the droplet size, the droplet size changes the feel and the release, and the release changes how much active reaches the skin. A good student project makes that interaction visible, because the whole system is small enough to hold in one head and test in one bench week. Years later, when I model surfactant assembly or score delivery systems on a rubric, I am working on the same interaction at a different scale.

The event was the sixth Volatile Oil Gallery at the Faculty of Pharmacy, Ain Shams University. A formulation award is judged on the product, so every weakness in the formula is a weakness in the entry. That is a useful discipline for a student: stability, standardisation of the natural ingredient and the choice of vehicle cannot be left as open questions, because the product has to stand as it is.

The award is listed with my other awards in my CV and on this site. It also marks the point at which formulation became more than a course for me. From 2019 onward I treated it as a field I wanted to work in, which led to the lung-targeted microparticle project the following year, to industrial formulation after graduation, and eventually to the question this site is organised around: whether the behaviour of a formulation can be predicted before it is made.
